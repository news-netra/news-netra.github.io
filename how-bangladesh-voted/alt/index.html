Bangladesh’s 2026 election

The map beneath the mandate

The BNP won 209 of 297 seats. But an analysis of all 42,382 polling-centre returns — matched to a voter roll of more than 120 million — finds a landslide assembled from electorates that wanted opposite things.

By Nazmul Ahasan Published Aug. 12, 2026 Six months after the vote
Featured illustration

Khulna-5, a coastal constituency at the heart of Bangladesh’s shrimp-farming industry, has long been unusual terrain for Jamaat-e-Islami. Roughly three in every 10 of its voters are Hindu, yet the Islamist party has historically done well there. In 2001, Jamaat’s secretary general, Mia Golam Parwar, a leading figure in its more hawkish wing, narrowly defeated the Awami League’s Hindu candidate.

Nearly a quarter of a century later, it was now Parwar who lost by a slender margin — to Ali Asgar Loby of the Bangladesh Nationalist Party, a tycoon and former cricket administrator. The margins were similar. The coalitions behind them were not.

Loby led in only eight of Khulna-5’s 18 unions, but his margins in those eight were large enough to overcome Parwar’s advantage in the other 10. Five of the eight were Hindu-majority, and together they gave the BNP a net advantage of 22,209 votes. Across the constituency’s other 13 unions, Jamaat led by 18,898. The arithmetic was simple: five Hindu-majority unions carried Loby over the line.

Turnout does not explain it. The five Hindu-majority unions voted at 75.8 per cent; the other 13 at 75.6. Whatever separated these places, it was not willingness to vote. Across the 18 unions the correlation between a union’s estimated Hindu share and the BNP’s vote share is 0.94.

The pattern extended beyond Khulna-5. In a counterfactual exercise — one in which Hindu-majority polling centres backed the Awami League, historically their preferred party, while every other vote remained unchanged — the BNP would have lost six seats.

In Pirojpur-2, for example, the BNP won about 80 per cent of the vote across 17 Hindu-majority centres, producing a margin without which its candidate would have been defeated. In several other constituencies, Hindu-majority centres supplied between a quarter and more than half of the BNP candidate’s winning margin.

Nationwide, at least 1,053 of the 36,164 polling centres we analysed appeared to have electorates that were more than half Hindu. The BNP carried more than 90 per cent of them. Jamaat won fewer than 1 per cent, with the remainder going to other candidates.

Indigenous-majority areas followed a different course. Across 290 such centres, candidates outside the BNP and Jamaat received 38.2 per cent of the vote, and independents led in 96. The difference was sharpest in Khagrachhari. In the 47 centres where Chakma names formed a majority of the matched voter roll, independents took 81.4 per cent, against 14.3 per cent for the BNP and 2.8 per cent for Jamaat. In 19 Tripura-majority centres, independents still received 55.7 per cent, but the BNP was considerably stronger, with 36.9 per cent; Jamaat received 4.6 per cent.

There was no single minority vote. Hindu-majority centres moved overwhelmingly into the BNP’s coalition. In the hills, much of the electorate preserved a politics outside both dominant parties.

These findings come from the first analysis of its kind of Bangladesh’s election results. Drawing on data from more than 40,000 polling centres and a voter roll of more than 120 million — matched, where possible, to the country’s smallest administrative units, from rural unions to municipalities and city corporations — the analysis reveals an election more complicated than the BNP’s sweeping victory suggests.

The BNP received 49.97 per cent of the valid vote and won 209 of the 297 declared seats. Jamaat received 31.76 per cent and won 68. The gap was decisive, but the electoral system enlarged it: just under half the vote gave the BNP more than 70 per cent of Parliament.

It exposes a divide between urban and rural Bangladesh. It complicates some of the most common assumptions about how women and young people voted. And by measuring each party’s strength at the local level, it offers an unusually detailed guide to their prospects in the coming elections for unions, municipalities, upazilas and city corporations.

Section illustration — participation

Part oneThe participation test

Because the Awami League was barred from contesting, turnout became an unusually important test of the election’s credibility. The Election Commission put it at 59.44 per cent nationally. But participation varied sharply according to where people lived — and how much appeared to be at stake.

Voters were also more likely to participate where the contest was close. Participation did not define the election’s overall result. But it was strongest where a ballot appeared most consequential — and weakest where the verdict looked foregone.

Section illustration — the young electorate

Part twoThe youth wave that wasn’t

Few pre-election assumptions proved more misleading than those about Bangladesh’s youngest voters.

Tens of millions of people — born in or after 1991 — had spent their entire adult lives waiting for an election like this one. In the 292 constituencies for which voter records were available, 44 million people — 36.1 per cent of the electorate — had been too young to vote in December 2008. Some had cast ballots in the elections that followed. None had witnessed a national contest widely regarded as genuinely competitive.

Before the election, Islami Chhatra Shibir, Jamaat’s student wing, swept student-union contests at universities across the country. The victories appeared to offer a preview of where this generation was heading. The national results told a very different story.

The BNP carried eight of the 10 constituencies with the highest concentrations of these voters. Khelafat Majlis and the National Citizen Party won the other two. Jamaat won none. Of the 20 unions with the highest concentrations, the BNP led in 18 and Jamaat in two. In Urirchar, a union in Sandwip where nearly half the electorate fell into that category, the BNP won 72 per cent of the vote to Jamaat’s 22 per cent.

The pattern is steady rather than dramatic. In the fifth of centres with the fewest young voters, the BNP led Jamaat by 23.4 points. In the fifth with the most, that lead fell to 16.3. Jamaat gained as the electorate got younger — but it did not lead anywhere along the range.

Shibir’s campus victories were real. They demonstrated the organisation’s appeal to a politically engaged segment of the urban young. But universities were not Bangladesh in miniature.

Section illustration — the women’s vote

Part threeThe women’s vote split at the city limits

Bangladesh’s women likely voted in smaller numbers than men. Across the 3,834 matched pairs of single-sex centres — a men’s centre and a women’s centre serving exactly the same houses — female turnout was lower in 81 per cent of them. Had women at those centres voted at the same rate as men, roughly 507,000 more ballots would have been cast.

The national result challenged one of the campaign’s most persistent assumptions: that women would broadly reject Jamaat. Across the 220 constituencies where single-sex centres cover at least a tenth of the roll, Jamaat took 32.8 per cent at women-only centres against 31.2 at men-only ones. The BNP remained well ahead among both, at roughly 49 per cent.

The assumption was not entirely wrong. It simply described urban women far better than women as a whole.

The divide persisted even when men-only and women-only centres at the same polling premises were matched. Meherpur-1 offered one of the starkest examples. About 19 per cent of its voters were registered at single-sex centres, split almost evenly between the two. At men-only centres, the BNP led Jamaat by two points, 50.4 per cent to 48.4. At women-only centres, the order reversed: Jamaat took 53.6 per cent to the BNP’s 45.5. Jamaat won the constituency.

The results broadly reflected the divide visible during the campaign. In Jamaat strongholds across the south-west, large rallies of burqa-clad women had revealed a constituency that many observers underestimated. Their presence was not merely theatrical. Women became an important part of Jamaat’s electoral coalition.

In the cities, the party encountered a different response. Jamaat fielded no women candidates. Its leaders said a woman would never lead the party and proposed limiting women’s working hours. Such positions alarmed many educated and working women. The urban results suggest that this fear was real — but concentrated far more heavily in the cities than across the country as a whole.

Nowhere was women’s support for Jamaat more consequential than in the south-west. Across the 29 constituencies in Khulna division covered by the analysis, Jamaat received 45.7 per cent at men-only centres. At women-only centres, its share rose to 50.7 per cent. The division accounted for 18 of Jamaat’s 47 victories in the gender sample.

The results do not suggest that Jamaat won women nationally; the BNP remained comfortably ahead at both male- and female-only centres. But women-only centres contributed substantially to Jamaat’s strength. In the cities, they helped contain its advance. Across the conservative towns and villages of the south-west, they helped turn religious appeal into parliamentary power.

Part fourThe election below the election

Bangladesh elected 297 lawmakers. But much of the power people encounter is exercised closer to home — through the union, municipality, upazila or city ward that determines which road is repaired, which drain is cleared and which development promise moves first.

Redrawn along those boundaries, the BNP’s parliamentary landslide did not disappear. It became thousands of smaller victories.

Jamaat’s parliamentary strength, in other words, was not confined to a few exceptional constituencies. The party held a leading position across a broad territorial base from which to contest the coming local elections.

But towns did not always follow their hinterlands. Among 306 upazila-level comparisons containing both municipal and union centres, 48 produced different leaders. In Badarganj, the rural unions gave Jamaat 53.8 per cent and the BNP 31.9; inside the municipality, the BNP led by 50 per cent to 41.5. Bakshiganj produced the reverse: the BNP carried the surrounding unions, while Jamaat won the municipality by 57 per cent to 39.4.

Such reversals reveal local fault lines that constituency results conceal. A party could dominate the villages surrounding a market town and still lose inside it — an especially important distinction as Bangladesh moves towards local elections.

The citywide results at first make Jamaat appear weaker. When all wards were combined, the BNP led 11 of the 12 city corporations. Jamaat led only Rangpur, where it carried 30 of the 33 mapped wards. But the ward map reveals a more complicated urban contest. Jamaat led 20 wards in Dhaka South, 19 in Dhaka North and 10 in Khulna. The BNP led every mapped ward in Chattogram and all but one in Cumilla. In Khulna, it led the corporation as a whole by fewer than two percentage points, while Jamaat carried nearly a third of its wards.

Independent candidates, meanwhile, became more visible as the map grew smaller. They won only seven parliamentary seats but led hundreds of unions. Their footprint all but vanished in the largest cities. These scattered strongholds mark the places where personal followings, family networks or established local organisations survived an election otherwise dominated by the BNP and Jamaat.

Part fiveThe map beneath the mandate

Six months on, the clearest lesson of the returns is not that the BNP’s victory was somehow less than decisive. It is that the victory was assembled from electorates that often wanted different things. The BNP built the country’s broadest coalition, absorbing Hindu-majority areas once associated with the Awami League and retaining an advantage across much of rural Bangladesh. Jamaat established itself as the national second force, but its appeal changed markedly between city and countryside, and between male- and female-only centres. In the hills, neither party fully contained the pull of regional candidates.

The parliamentary map flattened those distinctions. It could not show the towns that broke from their surrounding villages, the city wards that remained closely divided or the minority-majority centres that moved against the colour of the constituency around them. Those divisions will matter when national alliances give way to local candidates and local power.

Khulna-5 contains the election in miniature. The BNP lost most of its unions and won the constituency. Five Hindu-majority unions overcame Jamaat’s lead across the other 13 and turned two opposing local verdicts into one parliamentary result. From Dhaka, it looked like another piece of a landslide. On the ground, it was a victory measured in the distance between neighbouring places.

How we did this

The returns

The analysis uses the Election Commission’s published polling-centre returns for the 297 constituencies with declared results: 42,382 centres, 124.8 million registered voters and 73.5 million valid votes. Sherpur-3 did not vote on Feb. 12 after a candidate died. Chattogram-2 and Chattogram-4 were missing from the published centre returns, so centre-based measures exclude them.

The voter roll

The 2026 draft roll was published as roughly 200,000 PDF files, one per voter area, using an embedded font with a deliberately broken character map. We rebuilt a glyph table and decoded 125,104,801 records across 292 constituencies to correct Unicode Bengali; 99.996 per cent of rows are fully clean, and the remainder carry a visible marker rather than a silent error.

Joining the two

The result file’s centre identifier is not the polling-station database’s key. We matched on constituency and centre serial to the official station list, then to the six-digit voter-area codes that begin each voter number, then to the roll itself. Of 42,382 result centres, 36,164 clear a strict publication guard: a high-quality match, at least 500 registered voters and at least 500 linked roll rows. Those centres cover 85.6 per cent of the national register. Demographic figures use only those centres; result figures use all of them.

Inferring religion

Religion is not recorded on the roll. We classified names using a hand-labelled surname model, drawing on given, parent and spouse names to resolve ambiguous cases. Validated against the 2011 census across 419 well-covered upazilas, the inferred Hindu share correlates at 0.967 with the census share, with a voter-weighted mean absolute difference of 0.85 percentage points. The Hindu–Muslim classification is strong; the Christian and Buddhist classifiers are not, and no ranking of those communities is published here.

Places, not people

Every demographic result in this piece is ecological: it describes how places with a given composition voted, not how individuals voted. A centre where most registered voters have Hindu names voting heavily for one party does not establish that Hindu voters chose it. The ballot is secret, and nothing here recovers an individual choice.

Men’s and women’s centres

Bangladesh operates many single-sex polling centres. Where one men’s centre and one women’s centre served an identical set of voter-area codes, we treat them as a matched pair — 3,834 such pairs, in 278 constituencies. That design holds the neighbourhood constant. It does not make the sample nationally representative: single-sex centres are disproportionately urban, so village and town figures rest on a smaller share of those electorates and are reported separately throughout.

Ranking inside constituencies

Where the piece compares centres by age or composition, they are ranked into fifths within their own constituency before being pooled. Comparing centres across the whole country would mostly measure which constituency they sit in.

Local units

Parliamentary votes were aggregated into the boundaries of 4,539 unions, 319 municipalities, 463 city-corporation wards, 491 upazilas and 12 city corporations. These are the 2026 national votes redrawn along local borders — a baseline for local contests, not a forecast of them.

What we cannot say

The data cannot identify who abstained, who switched parties or where former Awami League voters went: no defensible constituency-level baseline from a comparable competitive election exists. It cannot recover the intention behind a rejected ballot. And it cannot attribute a vote to any individual — no voter-level data is published, and the smallest geography reported here averages about 22,000 registered voters.
