How Bangladesh Voted

The BNP won a landslide. Beneath it, cities, villages, women and minority-concentration areas produced markedly different elections.

KHULNA-5

Khulna-5, a coastal constituency at the heart of Bangladesh’s shrimp-farming industry, has long been unusual terrain for Jamaat-e-Islami. Roughly three in every 10 of its voters are Hindu, yet the Islamist party has historically done well there. In 2001, Jamaat’s secretary general, Mia Golam Parwar, a leading figure in its conservative wing, narrowly defeated the Awami League’s Hindu candidate.

This time, Parwar lost by another slender margin — to Ali Asgar Loby of the Bangladesh Nationalist Party, a tycoon and former cricket administrator. The margins were similar. The coalitions behind them were not.

Loby led in only eight of Khulna-5’s 18 unions, but his margins in those eight were large enough to overcome Parwar’s advantage in the other 10. Five of the eight were Hindu-majority, and together they gave the BNP a net advantage of 22,209 votes. Across the constituency’s other 13 unions, Jamaat led by 18,898. The arithmetic was simple: five Hindu-majority unions carried Loby over the line.

Eighteen local verdicts

The margin machine

Five Hindu-majority unions supplied a BNP advantage of 22,209 votes. The other 13 gave Jamaat an advantage of 18,898. What remained was a BNP victory.

The pattern extended beyond Khulna-5. In a counterfactual exercise — one in which Hindu-majority polling centres backed the Awami League, historically their preferred party, while every other vote remained unchanged — the BNP would have lost six seats.

In Pirojpur-2, for example, the BNP won about 80 per cent of the vote across 17 Hindu-majority centres, producing a margin without which its candidate would have been defeated. In several other constituencies, Hindu-majority centres supplied between a quarter and more than half of the BNP candidate’s winning margin.

Nationwide, at least 1,053 of the 36,164 polling centres we analysed appeared to have electorates that were more than half Hindu. The BNP carried more than 90 per cent of them. Jamaat won fewer than 1 per cent, with the remainder going to other candidates. The relationship was strikingly consistent: the greater the estimated concentration of Hindu voters at a polling centre, the smaller Jamaat’s share of the vote.

Indigenous-majority areas followed a different course. Across 290 such centres, candidates outside the BNP and Jamaat received 38.2 per cent of the vote, and independents led in 96. The difference was sharpest in Khagrachhari. In the 47 centres where Chakma names formed a majority of the matched voter roll, independents took 81.4 per cent, against 14.3 per cent for the BNP and 2.8 per cent for Jamaat. In 19 Tripura-majority centres, independents still received 55.7 per cent, but the BNP was considerably stronger, with 36.9 per cent; Jamaat received 4.6 per cent.

These findings come from the first analysis of its kind of Bangladesh’s election results. Drawing on data from more than 40,000 polling centres and a voter roll of more than 120 million — matched, where possible, to the country’s smallest administrative units, from rural unions to municipalities and city corporations — the analysis reveals an election more complicated than the BNP’s sweeping victory suggests.

The BNP received 49.97 per cent of the valid vote and won 209 of the 297 declared seats. Jamaat received 31.76 per cent and won 68. The gap was decisive, but the electoral system enlarged it: just under half the vote gave the BNP more than 70 per cent of Parliament.

It exposes a divide between urban and rural Bangladesh. It complicates some of the most common assumptions about how women and young people voted. And by measuring each party’s strength at the local level, it offers an unusually detailed guide to their prospects in the coming elections for unions, municipalities, upazilas and city corporations.

Participation

The participation test

Because the Awami League was barred from contesting, turnout became an unusually important test of the election’s credibility.

The Election Commission put it at 59.44 per cent nationally. But participation varied sharply according to where people lived — and how much appeared to be at stake.

Turnout in the centre returns was 61.8 per cent in rural unions and 59.9 per cent in municipalities. In city corporations, it fell to 47 per cent. The data cannot establish how many of those missing urban voters were abstaining Awami League supporters. Migration, outdated registration and the weaker pull of neighbourhood political networks may also have kept city voters away. What it establishes is that the election drew a substantially thinner public in the country’s largest urban centres.

Voters were more likely to participate where the contest was close. Turnout averaged 61.8 per cent in the closest fifth of constituencies, compared with 57.3 per cent in the most lopsided fifth. In the eight seats decided by less than one percentage point, it reached 64.1 per cent. Participation did not define the election’s overall result. But it was strongest where a ballot appeared most consequential — and weakest where the verdict looked foregone.

A generation’s first contest

The youth wave that wasn’t

Few pre-election assumptions proved more misleading than those about Bangladesh’s youngest voters.

Tens of millions of people — born in or after 1991 — had spent their entire adult lives waiting for an election like this one. In the 292 constituencies for which voter records were available, 44 million people — 36.1 per cent of the electorate — had been too young to vote in December 2008. Some had cast ballots in the elections that followed. None had witnessed a national contest widely regarded as genuinely competitive.

Before the election, Islami Chhatra Shibir, Jamaat’s student wing, swept student-union contests at universities across the country. The victories appeared to offer a preview of where this generation was heading. The national results told a very different story.

The BNP carried eight of the 10 constituencies with the highest concentrations of these voters. Khelafat Majlis and the National Citizen Party won the other two. Jamaat won none.

The pattern held closer to the ground. Of the 20 unions with the highest concentrations of voters who had been too young to take part in 2008, the BNP led in 18 and Jamaat in two. In Urirchar, a union in Sandwip where nearly half the electorate fell into that category, the BNP won 72 per cent of the vote to Jamaat’s 22 per cent.

The polling-centre results, however, add nuance. Within each constituency, we ranked centres by their concentration of these younger voters. In the 7,296 centres in the highest quintile, the BNP took 48.9 per cent of the vote and Jamaat 32.6. In the 7,347 centres in the lowest quintile, the split was 52.9 per cent to 29.5. The BNP did better where younger voters were least concentrated.

Shibir’s victories at public universities were consistent with another pattern in the data: Jamaat ran the BNP more closely at younger-heavy centres in cities than at their counterparts in the countryside. Where both parties fielded candidates, the BNP led Jamaat by only 2.6 percentage points in the fifth of city polling centres with the highest concentrations of younger voters. Its lead widened to 4.7 points across 300 comparable centres in municipalities, and to 7.1 points across 4,537 rural ones. The further from the big cities, the wider the BNP’s lead at these younger-heavy centres.

Shibir’s campus victories were real. They demonstrated the organisation’s appeal to a politically engaged segment of the urban young. But universities were not Bangladesh in miniature.

Same places, different electorates

The women’s vote split at the city limits

Bangladesh’s women likely voted in smaller numbers than men. Their centres also produced opposite partisan effects in the countryside and the largest cities.

Across the 3,834 matched pairs of single-sex centres, female turnout was lower in 81 per cent of them. Had women at those centres voted at the same rate as men, roughly 507,000 more ballots would have been cast. The gap was widest where Hindu voters were most concentrated: about four percentage points in areas with few Hindu voters, and nearly 10 in areas conservatively identified as Hindu-majority.

At women-only centres, geography reversed the pattern seen at younger-heavy ones. Jamaat’s relative performance at younger-heavy centres was strongest in the cities. Its advantage at women-only centres came instead from villages and small towns.

The national result alone challenged one of the campaign’s most persistent assumptions: that women would broadly reject Jamaat. Across the single-sex polling centres we analysed, Jamaat received 32.8 per cent of the vote at women-only centres, compared with 31.2 per cent at men-only ones. The BNP remained well ahead among both, with slightly more than 49 per cent.

The assumption was not entirely wrong. It simply described urban women far better than women as a whole.

The south-west

Across Khulna division, the gap was far larger: 45.7 per cent at men-only centres and 50.7 per cent at women-only centres.

These comparisons do not amount to a conventional survey of men and women. Single-sex centres are not distributed evenly across the country. Urban areas account for only about 10 per cent of the electorate, compared with roughly 80 per cent in rural areas, yet cities contain a disproportionately large share of single-sex centres. That makes the urban comparison the more reliable one. The rural results, drawn from a much smaller share of rural voters, should be read more cautiously.

Still, the divide persisted even when men-only and women-only centres at the same polling premises were matched. Across 586 matched village premises, women shifted the contest 0.8 percentage points towards Jamaat. Across 127 premises in municipalities, they shifted it 3.1 points towards Jamaat. But across 491 big-city premises, women moved the contest three points towards the BNP.

Meherpur-1 offered one of the starkest examples. About 19 per cent of its voters were registered at single-sex centres, split almost evenly between the two, providing an unusually clear, if imperfect, view of how men and women voted. At men-only centres, the BNP led Jamaat by two points, 50.4 per cent to 48.4. At women-only centres, the order reversed: Jamaat took 53.6 per cent to the BNP’s 45.5. Jamaat won the constituency.

The same pattern appeared in Meherpur-2. Men-only centres divided almost evenly between the two parties. At women-only centres, Jamaat led by 10 percentage points, helping it win the seat.

The results broadly reflected the divide visible during the campaign. In Jamaat strongholds across the south-west, large rallies of burqa-clad women had revealed a constituency that many observers underestimated. Their presence was not merely theatrical. Women became an important part of Jamaat’s electoral coalition.

In the cities, the party encountered a different response. Jamaat fielded no women candidates. Its leaders said a woman would never lead the party and proposed limiting women’s working hours. Such positions alarmed many educated and working women. The urban results are consistent with those concerns — but show that the response was concentrated far more heavily in the cities than across the country as a whole.

Nowhere was women’s support for Jamaat more consequential than in the south-west. Across the 29 constituencies in Khulna division covered by the analysis, Jamaat received 45.7 per cent at men-only centres. At women-only centres, its share rose to 50.7 per cent.

The division accounted for 18 of Jamaat’s 47 victories in the gender sample. In every one of those seats, Jamaat performed better at women-only centres than at men-only ones, by an average of more than six percentage points. In Jessore-1, its share rose from 54.6 per cent among men to 63.9 among women. In Jhenaidah-3, from 52.9 to 61.1. In Satkhira-2, where Jamaat was already dominant, from 64.2 to 70.3.

Across the constituencies covered, Jamaat’s vote share was higher at women-only centres in 43 of the 47 seats it won. In six — including Meherpur-1, Bagerhat-4 and Nilphamari-3 — men-only centres preferred the BNP while women-only centres preferred Jamaat.

The results do not suggest that Jamaat won women nationally; the BNP remained comfortably ahead at both male- and female-only centres. But women-only centres contributed substantially to Jamaat’s strength. In the cities, they helped contain its advance. Across the conservative towns and villages of the south-west, they helped turn religious appeal into parliamentary power.

The elections to come

The election below the election

Bangladesh elected 297 lawmakers. But much of the power people encounter is exercised closer to home.

Through the union, municipality, upazila or city ward, local power determines which road is repaired, which drain is cleared and which development promise moves first.

Redrawn along those boundaries, the BNP’s parliamentary landslide did not disappear. It became thousands of smaller victories.

The BNP also received the most votes in 3,058 of the 4,539 unions we mapped, 227 of 319 municipalities, 357 of 491 upazilas and 338 of 463 city-corporation wards. At every tier, its reach closely resembled its command of Parliament: the BNP led 67 per cent of unions, 71 per cent of municipalities and roughly 73 per cent of both upazilas and city wards.

Jamaat’s map was nearly as consistent, though substantially smaller. It led 23 per cent of unions, 21 per cent of municipalities and upazilas, and 22 per cent of city wards.

Jamaat’s parliamentary strength, in other words, was not confined to a few exceptional constituencies. The party held a leading position in 1,037 unions, 67 municipalities, 102 upazilas and 104 city wards — a broad territorial base from which to contest the coming local elections.

But towns did not always follow their hinterlands. Among 306 upazila-level comparisons containing both municipal and union centres, 48 produced different leaders.

Such reversals reveal local fault lines that constituency results conceal. A party could dominate the villages surrounding a market town and still lose inside it — an especially important distinction as Bangladesh moves towards local elections.

The citywide results at first make Jamaat appear weaker. When all wards were combined, the BNP led 11 of the 12 city corporations. Jamaat led only Rangpur, where it carried 30 of the 33 mapped wards.

But the ward map reveals a more complicated urban contest. Jamaat led 20 wards in Dhaka South, 19 in Dhaka North and 10 in Khulna. The BNP led every mapped ward in Chattogram and all but one in Cumilla. In Khulna, it led the corporation as a whole by fewer than two percentage points, while Jamaat carried nearly a third of its wards.

Independent candidates, meanwhile, became more visible as the map grew smaller. They won only seven parliamentary seats but led 205 unions, 13 municipalities and 15 upazilas. Their footprint all but vanished in the largest cities, where an independent led just one of the 463 mapped wards.

These scattered strongholds matter. They mark the places where personal followings, family networks or established local organisations survived an election otherwise dominated by the BNP and Jamaat. Smaller parties produced similar pockets: the NCP led 71 unions and 12 city wards, while Khelafat candidates led 76 unions and six city wards.

The map also shows how much remains unsettled. The leading party finished fewer than five percentage points ahead in 643 unions, 49 municipalities and 75 upazilas. Eighty-nine city wards — nearly one in five — were that close.

These are not predictions of what will happen locally. Parliamentary alliances will fracture; both major parties may face rebel candidates; and voters who followed a party nationally may prefer a locally prominent leader. The governing party may also gain an advantage from promises of roads, services and access to development money. This map is a baseline, not a replica of the elections to come. But it identifies the places where those contests may begin most fiercely.

Conclusion

The map beneath the mandate

Six months on, the clearest lesson of the returns is not that the BNP’s victory was somehow less than decisive. It is that the victory was assembled from electorates that often wanted different things.

The BNP built the country’s broadest coalition, absorbing Hindu-majority areas once associated with the Awami League and retaining an advantage across much of rural Bangladesh. Jamaat established itself as the national second force, but its appeal changed markedly between city and countryside, and between male- and female-only centres. In the hills, neither party fully contained the pull of regional candidates.

The parliamentary map flattened those distinctions. It could not show the towns that broke from their surrounding villages, the city wards that remained closely divided or the minority-majority centres that moved against the colour of the constituency around them. Those divisions will matter when national alliances give way to local candidates and local power.

Khulna-5 contains the election in miniature. The BNP lost most of its unions and won the constituency. Five Hindu-majority unions overcame Jamaat’s lead across the other 13 and turned two opposing local verdicts into one parliamentary result. From Dhaka, it looked like another piece of a landslide. On the ground, it was a victory measured in the distance between neighbouring places.

Methodology+

What we analysed

The analysis uses published returns from 42,382 polling centres in the 297 constituencies with declared results. Voter-roll files cover 292 constituencies. After duplicate voter numbers were removed, they contained 122.3 million entries.

How centres were connected

Polling centres were matched to voter areas through official centre numbers and six-digit voter-area codes. Strict demographic findings use 36,164 centres in 285 constituencies where the match passed the publication guard. Centres were also joined to unions, municipalities, upazilas, city corporations and city wards.

Religion, ethnicity and age

Religion and Indigenous composition were inferred conservatively from diagnostic names in the roll and checked at aggregate level. Chakma and Tripura centre classifications use explicit family-name signals. Age comes from dates of birth. All comparisons are ecological: they describe places, not the secret ballot of any individual voter.

Women and men

National comparisons use single-sex polling centres. The stricter test pairs male and female centres serving identical voter-area codes or the same polling premises. These comparisons hold geography more constant, but they are not a survey and single-sex centres are unevenly distributed.

Counterfactuals and local elections

The six-seat Awami League scenario is mechanical: votes at Hindu-majority centres are reassigned while all other centre results remain fixed. It is not a prediction. Parliamentary results at local administrative levels are also baselines, not forecasts; candidate fields, alliances and incentives will change.

Known gaps

A handful of unions could not be mapped. Sylhet City Corporation is under-represented because its recent expansion was not captured in the 2021 administrative geometry. The Election Commission’s 59.44 per cent figure is used as official national turnout; centre-sheet calculations are used for internally consistent geographic comparisons.
